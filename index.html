The perspective

Why move the camera to the pedestrian

Almost all crossing-intention prediction is exocentric. It informs the vehicle and presupposes an instrumented vehicle or roadside. Predicting from the pedestrian's own view changes not only the input but who the prediction is for.

Vehicle-side prediction

None

Data come from a virtual reality (VR) shared-space experiment with synchronized first-person video, eye tracking, and trajectories. VR is what makes the gaze observable at all and lets near-miss encounters be staged rather than waited for. It also means the accuracies below are comparative evidence under controlled conditions, not field performance.

Methodology

From first-person video to crossing intention

Given the past 2 seconds of the pedestrian’s view, predict whether they will cross or yield in the next second.

Evaluate on unseen participants

Split participants 60% / 10% / 30% into training, validation, and test sets. Select checkpoints on validation accuracy and report test accuracy and macro F1 across three training seeds. Input-corruption tests probe cue use; MMBench checks general visual reasoning after adaptation.

Labels and training settings

Windows advance in 0.5-second steps. Labels use near-term movement as a proxy for intention: “cross” means speed exceeds the crossing threshold for most of the next second; otherwise the label is “yield.” The dataset contains 2,486 cross and 3,561 yield samples.

LoRA uses rank 2, scaling factor 8, and AdamW with a learning rate of 0.0001 for up to 10 epochs. We select the checkpoint with the highest validation accuracy for each run.

Results

Four things the experiments showed

01

Fine-tuning closes the accuracy gap

62.9%best zero-shot accuracy · video only
Qwen2.5-VL-7B

78.8%best fine-tuned accuracy · video only
VLP (GroundVQA)

  • Zero-shot: the best video-only model reaches 62.9% accuracy. No zero-shot model outperforms CLIP+Transformer baseline.
  • Fine-tuned: Qwen3-VL-2B reaches 77.5% and VLP reaches 78.8%. Both exceed the task-specific baseline.
  • Prompting: Both chain-of-thought and visual markers falls short while adding heavy latency (3–5 seconds per sample)

Results use egocentric video only, without additional context. The alternative prompts use Qwen2.5-VL-7B: chain-of-thought asks for intermediate reasoning; set-of-marks highlights the shuttle and crossing goal. Hover a bar for its accuracy, macro F1, and inference time.

02

Gaze helps most after fine-tuning

83.5%best fine-tuned accuracy · video + context
Qwen3-VL-2B

  • Zero-shot: gaze context alone lowers accuracy. Ego motion helps; ego + on-screen gaze gives the highest score, 66.3% (+2.7 points).
  • Fine-tuned: ego + gaze direction gives the largest gain, +6.8 points over no context.
  • Motion cues: ego motion alone adds 2.6 points zero-shot and 3.7 after fine-tuning. Vehicle motion adds little beyond ego motion.
The best fine-tuned configuration improves accuracy by 14.9% relative to CLIP+Transformer and 7.7% relative to the same model fine-tuned on raw video alone.

Bars show accuracy changes in percentage points. Left of zero means lower accuracy; right means higher. Both “Cue alone” and “Ego + cue” use the same no-text-context baseline within each panel. All configurations use gaze-overlaid video.

03

Evidence against shortcut learning

48.3%accuracy without video · down from 77.5%
Qwen3-VL-2B · video-only fine-tuning

  • Visual evidence: removing, blanking, or mismatching frames drops video-only accuracy below the 56.7% majority-class baseline.
  • Temporal order: reversing frames hurts more than shuffling them, suggesting the model uses motion direction.
  • Gaze + context: corrupted frames have less effect. Withholding context drops accuracy from 83.5% to 65.4%, even with the original video.

    What the corruption tests establish

    The video-only results support reliance on visual evidence rather than language bias alone. They do not rule out every possible shortcut. The context-guided model also uses the supplied motion and gaze measurements, so robustness to frame corruption does not by itself indicate a shortcut.

    General ability varies across training runs

    We test whether adapting to crossing prediction preserves general visual reasoning on MMBench.

    • Ego + gaze: outcomes vary widely despite the same training settings, from modest losses to substantial degradation.
    • Model selection: crossing accuracy alone is insufficient; each checkpoint also needs a general-reasoning evaluation.

    Implications
    
    
    What follows for practice
    
    
    
      
    
        
    Gaze-capable eyewear, not a forward camera alone
    
        
    Of every cue we tested, gaze yields the largest gain and vehicle motion the smallest. A device is worth what it senses.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    A complement to vehicle-centric sensing
    
        
    The model anticipates the wearer's own decision from what a head-mounted device already captures, with no instrumented vehicle or infrastructure, and it covers the occlusion cases vehicle sensors cannot.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Training stability is the deployment target
    
        
    Gaze-guided adaptation can keep general capability or destroy it depending on the seed. Checkpoint selection on a general held-out set, or a rehearsal term, are the next things to try.
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    Read with these limits
    
      
      
        
    • VR data. Behaviour in VR has not been validated against real pedestrian–AV encounters, because no such recordings exist.
      • 
        
    • Uniform frame sampling. Brief but decisive cues can fall between frames.
      • 
        
    • On-device operation is a design constraint here, not a demonstrated result.
